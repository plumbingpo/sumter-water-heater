A cold shower from a gas water heater usually means one thing: the burner is not firing, and something told it not to. Gas heaters are built around safety. The control valve will not open unless a thermocouple or flame sensor proves there is a flame, and many models lock out if the flame arrestor screen at the bottom gets clogged or the heater senses spilled exhaust. So the real question is which safety did its job, and why. Across Sumter, SC, the answer is most often a worn thermocouple, a dirty pilot or burner, a lint-choked air intake, a failing gas valve, or a vent that is no longer drafting properly.

Sumter Water Heater does not guess with gas appliances. On every Sumter call, our technicians test the thermocouple's millivolt output, inspect the pilot and burner flame, check gas pressure at the valve, and run a draft test at the draft hood to confirm exhaust is actually leaving the house. We look inside the combustion chamber for scorching, rust, and signs of flame rollout, which tell us whether the flue is blocked or the burner is misbehaving, and we check for carbon monoxide around the heater. One rule comes before all of it: if you smell gas, do not light anything or flip switches. Leave the house, call your gas utility from outside, and then call us once it is safe.

Most gas water heater repairs are finished in a single visit. We replace thermocouples, flame sensors, pilot assemblies, and igniters, clean burners and flame arrestor screens, swap failed gas control valves, and correct vent connectors that have separated or lost their slope. We also flush sediment, the cause of that popping or rumbling sound and a major reason tanks overheat at the bottom and fail early. When the tank itself is leaking or the heater is deep into its service life, we tell you honestly and walk you through replacement. New installations are sized by first-hour rating, not just gallons, and include the right venting, a sediment trap on the gas line, an expansion tank where needed, and seismic straps and a drain pan where code requires them. Warning Signs to Watch For

A heater that is starting to struggle rarely hides it for long, and the clues are easy to spot once you know them. Keep an eye out for the following:

  • A rotten-egg odor near the water heater, in which case get out and call the gas company first
  • A silent heater with no flame and no hot water
  • Rust-colored hot water or rust streaks on the tank
  • A pilot that lights, then goes out when you release the knob
  • Showers that go cold sooner than they did a year ago
  • A vent pipe that has separated, rusted, or left scorch marks near the top of the heater
  • Soot or scorch marks around the burner access door
  • Loud rumbling from the bottom of the tank

What a Trained Technician Brings to the Job

Consider the risks first. Every gas water heater involves fuel piping, an open flame, and exhaust that contains carbon monoxide. A loose flare or threaded joint can leak gas, a vent that is disconnected or poorly sloped can spill exhaust back into the room, and a gas valve installed without a leak test is a risk you cannot see. Then there is the diagnostic side: a pilot that goes out can mean a weak thermocouple, a dirty pilot orifice, a clogged flame arrestor, a draft problem, or a failing gas valve, and working through them by guesswork is slow and costly.

Professional service gets you a correct answer and a safe appliance. Instead of trial and error, we confirm thermocouple output, gas pressure, burner flame, and draft with proper tools. Every gas connection we make is tested for leaks before we leave, and vent connectors are secured with the right slope and clearances. When a replacement is needed, we size the new heater by first-hour rating, handle the permit where one is required, and add the expansion tank, sediment trap, drain pan, or seismic straps your home needs. Tips to Keep Your Water Heater Healthy

Routine maintenance is almost always cheaper and easier than an early replacement. Put these technician-approved steps to work at home:

  • Drain sediment from the bottom of the tank once or twice a year
  • Keep working CO alarms near bedrooms and on each floor
  • Keep the area around the heater clear, especially of gasoline, paint, and solvents
  • Vacuum lint and dust from the flame arrestor screen at the base a few times a year
  • Set the gas valve near 120 degrees, because hotter water costs more and can scald
  • Replace a spent anode rod before the tank itself starts to rust
  • Make sure the vent pipe stays connected, sloped upward, and free of rust
